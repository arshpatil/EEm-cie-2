The paper maps forecasting, risk, fuzzy reasoning, recommendations, engineering economics, and user-experience research into a proposed framework for helping people compare major purchases. It is a synthesis and design blueprint, not evidence that one complete consumer financial-advice system has been built and validated.

01 / The decision problem

Why consumer purchase planning is difficult

The survey frames a household purchase as an uncertain, multi-criteria decision. The same person must make sense of changing conditions, future cash flows, and priorities that resist a single neat score.

A≈

Markets move

Prices, inflation, interest rates, and income can shift while a person is planning. Historical patterns may not describe a non-stationary future.

Forecasting context [2]
B∿

Cash flows are uncertain

A home, vehicle, or retirement plan unfolds over years. The timing and size of payments, returns, and available cash all matter.

C⌘

Signals do not line up

Budgets, financing terms, market prices, reviews, and qualitative preferences come in different formats and levels of certainty.

People have limited attention

Static spreadsheets and rigid tools can leave users to assemble the analysis themselves, increasing information load and obscuring trade-offs.

02 / Engineering-economics toolkit

Translate the choice into money over time.

The paper’s central bridge is engineering economics: make future costs comparable, express competing priorities, and examine how a result changes when assumptions move.

TIME VALUE OF MONEY · TVMSurvey equation (1) [2]
NPV = Σt=0n CFt(1 + r)t

Discount each future cash flow back to today. A positive cash flow later is worth less today when the discount rate is positive.

CFt net cash flow in period tr discount raten time horizon
INTERNAL RATE OF RETURN · IRR
0 = Σ CFt(1 + IRR)t

The rate at which the discounted cash flows break even. Compare it with an appropriate alternative rate; it is not a guarantee of return.

COST–BENEFIT
Net benefit = benefits − costs

List the gains and outlays that belong in the decision, then make timing and omitted costs visible.

PRICE–VALUE TRADE-OFF
PVi = Utilityi − Costi

UTAUT 2’s price-value idea describes a person’s perceived utility relative to monetary cost [3].

MULTI-CRITERIA DECISION · MCDM
Score(option) = Σ wi si

Combine criteria such as affordability, security, value, and satisfaction. AHP can derive normalized weights from pairwise importance judgments.

RISK & SENSITIVITY
Δ result / Δ assumption

Vary rates, prices, returns, or income assumptions to see where a plan becomes fragile. Fuzzy rules can represent linguistic uncertainty [1].

BUDGET VARIANCE
(Actual − planned) / planned

Track the percentage difference between actual and planned spending; expose when an option may strain available cash.

Equations and concepts are paraphrased from the supplied survey. The simplified examples below explain the arithmetic; they are not the survey’s empirical results.

How this example is calculated

NPV = −initial cost + the present value of each annual benefit over the selected horizon. IRR is the annual rate that makes that same cash-flow sequence’s NPV equal zero. Taxes, inflation, financing, and other costs are not included.

05 / Open problems

Three gaps between model output and a useful decision.

The survey’s synthesis points to a mismatch: technically capable methods may still fail to represent consumer behavior, preserve meaning, or support human judgment.

GAP 01
Corporate goals≠Consumer priorities

Corporate-to-consumer behavioral gap

Methods built for corporate managers and objective measures may not reflect trust, enjoyment, personal priorities, or the way households actually adopt a tool.

Survey synthesis drawing on [1] and [3].
GAP 02
“Moderately stable”→?

Semantic nuance gets flattened

Rigid numerical inputs can lose nuance in contracts and everyday language. Static, hand-built fuzzy rules may be difficult to keep current and can encode bias.

Survey synthesis drawing on [1] and [2].
GAP 03
!attention

Fast visuals can still overload

Visualization research often optimizes latency and scale. A consumer interface also needs to account for attention, cognitive effort, and the risk of information overload.

Survey synthesis drawing on [5] and [6].

Interpretation. These are research gaps articulated by the survey authors, not resolved findings from a single common experiment.

06 / Future research

Promising directions, still proposals.

The survey suggests research paths for addressing the gaps. The ideas below are not established capabilities or tested components of a deployed consumer system.

↗PROPOSED RESEARCH DIRECTIONS · NOT VALIDATED PRODUCT FEATURES
01HYBRID MODELING

Join deep learning with fuzzy reasoning

Explore architectures such as SEM–ANN or GMM–fuzzy systems to combine non-linear patterns with qualitative perceptions and uncertain inputs.

Research questionsHow are membership rules learned and audited?Do hybrids generalize beyond the source datasets?
02LANGUAGE & RULES

Use NLP to support fuzzy-rule discovery

Investigate financial language models such as FinBERT and semantic encoders to extract candidate variables from contracts, advice, and market text.

Research questionsHow are source, uncertainty, and context preserved?How are errors and bias reviewed by people?
03HUMAN-CENTERED VISUALIZATION

Adapt detail to cognitive load

Study interfaces that change visual density in response to user needs. The survey discusses interaction, eye-tracking, or EEG signals as possibilities, not deployed features.

Research questionsCan adaptive displays improve decision quality?Which signals can be gathered with clear consent?

Source. These directions summarize Section VI of the supplied survey. Their presence here does not imply that they have been empirically validated for consumer purchase planning.
